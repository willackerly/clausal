# Boundary-recovery benchmark: results for hypothesis H1

Generated by `bench/score.py` from `out/bench/raw.jsonl` (written by `Rosette/Bench.lean`). All numbers below are computed from that file. Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over items (2000 resamples).

## What was measured

For each ground-truth pair (theorem T, helper L) of `bench/corpus.json`, L's proof is inlined into T's proof term: every maximal application `L a1 ... an` is replaced by L's proof value with universe parameters instantiated and the arguments substituted and beta-reduced. The constant L no longer occurs. Every candidate lemma boundary (Prop-typed subterm with DAG size at least 20) of the flattened term is enumerated, and we ask where the inlined copy ranks under each scoring rule.

**Ground-truth target.** The target is the subterm that the application `L a1 ... an` turned into, i.e. the *instantiated* body, identified by its tree position (recorded during inlining with a private marker that is erased before enumeration). Its closed statement is `forall (the local variables it uses), (L's conclusion at those arguments)`, which is usually not syntactically L's statement: L is stated for arbitrary arguments, the inlined copy only for the ones T passes. This is the fair target, because no subterm of the flattened proof has L's general statement; requiring it would make the task unsolvable by construction. If L is used several times, the best-ranked copy counts. If L was under-applied directly under a lambda, its leading lambdas merge with the enclosing telescope and the target is the telescope body (same closed statement).

**Candidates.** Candidates with the same closed statement (structural hash) and the same proof and statement sizes are one candidate: they are the same lemma under every rule. The enumeration is DAG-aware (a subterm already visited with the same free variables is not visited again); see the caveat on `Rosette.Candidates` at the end.

**Ranks.** Rank 1 is best. Ties get the mid-rank (expected rank under random tie-breaking), so a top-1 hit requires a strictly best score. Normalised rank is (rank - 1) / (pool size - 1): 0 is best, 0.5 is what a random order gives for a single target.

## Leakage and triviality checks

- All rules use only three numbers per candidate (proof size, statement size, number of dependencies). They cannot see names, universe parameters, metadata or term shape, so artifacts could only matter through sizes.
- `mdata` is erased from both proofs before inlining (present in 105 theorem proofs and 119 helper proofs); flattened terms still containing `mdata`: 0; still mentioning the constant L: 0.
- Beta-redexes: the inlined application is beta-reduced, including redexes created when an argument that is a lambda lands in head position. Helper proofs that already contained redexes before inlining: 136; theorem proofs: 121 (so a redex is not a marker of the inlined region). Items where the flattened term has more distinct redexes than T and L had together (redexes created deeper than head position): 20.
- Universe parameters of L are instantiated with the levels T used. Binder names inside the inlined body are L's own; no rule reads them.
- Sizes are DAG sizes of the instantiated terms, so an argument substituted many times is counted once. Candidate sizes are measured with bound variables replaced by local variables, which shares more than the de Bruijn form; the `dag` field of `targets` in results.jsonl is the de Bruijn size and is larger.
- Triviality: in 19 of 323 items the inlined helper is the largest non-root candidate (nothing but the whole proof is bigger), and in 19 its closed statement is the theorem's own statement (T is a direct wrapper of L). Results are split on this below. Median share of the flattened proof taken by the inlined helper: 41%.

**H1 is not supported as stated.** In plain terms:

1. *The human boundary does rank high, but it is rarely the first pick.* Over 323 theorems (median 112 candidates each), hidingRatio puts the human helper at a median normalised rank of 0.049 (roughly the top 5% of candidates) and in the top 5 for 47% of theorems, against 9% for a random order. It is the strict first pick in only 7.7% of theorems. A tool that lifted the single best-scoring subterm would reproduce the human's lemma about one time in 13.
2. *It does not clearly beat the simplest baseline.* Ranking by proof size alone gets top-5 41% and MRR 0.229, versus 47% and 0.257 for hidingRatio. The paired MRR difference is +0.028 [+0.008, +0.049], top-5 +6.2 points [+1.5, +10.5], top-1 +1.9 points [-0.3, +4.0]: On MRR hidingRatio is higher (interval excludes 0); on top-5 it is higher (interval excludes 0); on top-1 it is not distinguishable (interval includes 0). Where the difference is real it is a few points, not a different regime: most of the signal is 'big subterms are lemma-like', and dividing by statement size is a small correction to that.
3. *Statement size alone and dependency count alone carry no signal.* 1/statementSize (top-5 7%) and fewest dependencies (top-5 3%) are at or below random (9%), with median normalised ranks of 0.57 and 0.68 (0.5 is chance). The 'small working-memory state' idea, taken alone as a ranking rule, does not point at human boundaries.
4. *Fitted combinations do not rescue it.* Held-out MRR in pool `noRoot`: log P - lambda * log S 0.260 (lambda = 1 is exactly hidingRatio, lambda = 0 is proofSize), log P - lambda * S 0.234, MDL-style saving 0.225, against 0.257 for hidingRatio. Relative to log P - lambda * log S, hidingRatio's MRR is not distinguishable (interval includes 0); relative to the MDL saving it is higher (interval excludes 0). No tested per-candidate formula of these three numbers does better than the plain ratio by more than noise.
5. *The triviality split matters.* In the 19 theorems where the inlined helper is the largest non-root candidate, proofSize is right by construction and hidingRatio is right 79% of the time. In the other 304, hidingRatio's strict top-1 rate is 3.3% (proofSize: 0.0%, necessarily, because something bigger exists), top-5 45% versus 37%.

**What the failures have in common.**

- *The winner is usually the helper plus some of its surroundings.* In 275 of 323 theorems hidingRatio's top candidate is a chunk that encloses the human helper (proofSize: 304). In those 275 cases the enclosing winner's proof is a median 1.7 times the helper's, its statement is no larger than the helper's in 81% of them, and in 96% its statement is simply the theorem's own statement (the proof body after introducing the theorem's variables). Moving outward from a good lemma the proof grows while the statement does not grow in proportion, so the ratio keeps rising; a score computed per candidate from sizes alone has no way to say 'stop here'.
- *Small helpers lose.* Helpers found in the top 5 take a median 55% of the flattened proof; missed ones 28%. Missed helpers also have larger instantiated statements (median 235 nodes versus 149) and sit in proofs with more candidates (median 162 versus 70).
- *Instantiated statements are long.* After inlining, the helper is stated at T's specific arguments, with every local variable it touches as a binder. Human helpers with ratio below 1 at their use site exist (examples 5 to 8): whatever made the human cut there, it is not visible in these sizes. (Possible reasons, none measured here: the statement is short only in its general form, the helper is used twice, or it keeps the caller readable.)
- *If enclosing chunks are removed by oracle*, hidingRatio's top-1 rises to 58% and top-5 to 76% (proofSize: 68% and 79%). So the dominant failure is the choice of *extent* along one nested chain, not confusion with unrelated parts of the proof. In that pool hidingRatio's MRR is lower (interval excludes 0) relative to proof size alone: once the enclosing chain is out of the way, dividing by statement size does not help. This pool uses the ground truth and is not an achievable result.
- *Filters help top-1 but cost recall.* Dropping restatements of the theorem gives top-1 16.1%; the full `Rosette.Lift` filter gives top-1 18.6% but excludes the human helper itself in 45 theorems (ground truth restates the theorem: 19; is a typeclass-instance statement: 14; is more than 80% of the flattened proof: 17; a theorem can fall in more than one group).

**What this does and does not show.** hidingRatio is a usable *shortlisting* signal: the human boundary is typically in the top few percent of candidates. It is not a boundary *selector*: it does not single out the human's cut, and it is only slightly better than proof size. The benchmark scores agreement with one human-chosen boundary per theorem; it cannot tell whether the enclosing chunk that out-ranks it would have been an equally good lemma. A rule that compares a candidate with its own parent and children along the nesting chain (where does the ratio stop improving?), or a global objective over a set of cuts, is the obvious next thing to test; neither was tested here.

## Caveats

- The corpus contains only helpers that Mathlib authors marked as such (private or `aux`-named), one per module. It says nothing about unmarked lemma boundaries.
- The ground truth is one boundary per theorem. A candidate that out-ranks it is not thereby a bad lemma: other good boundaries may exist in the same proof and are counted as errors here. The benchmark measures agreement with one human choice, not lemma quality.
- Inlining removes a boundary that the human had already cut out, and the surrounding proof of T was written around that boundary. Whether flattened proofs resemble proofs written without the helper is not tested.
- Enumeration here is `Rosette.Bench.enumVisit`, a DAG-aware copy of `Rosette.visit` with the same path conventions and the same per-candidate computation. The tree walk in `Rosette.Candidates` hit its budgets (20,000 candidates / 400,000 visited subterms) on 3 of the first 8 items, because flattened proofs share large subterms. `Rosette/Candidates.lean` itself was not modified.
- The statement of an inlined copy is the type Lean infers for the instantiated proof body, not L's declared statement at those arguments. The two are definitionally equal but can differ syntactically (item 257 among the worked failures: the inferred statement has the form `a = a`). Statement sizes of ground truths can therefore differ from the size of the human-written statement at those arguments; how often was not measured.
- Candidates below 20 DAG nodes, non-Prop subterms, and applications of a constant or variable to atoms are not candidates (the `Rosette.Candidates` defaults).
- The headline sentences of the verdict ('not supported as stated', the five numbered headings) are fixed text in `bench/score.py`, written for this run's numbers; the figures and the 'higher / lower / not distinguishable' readings inside them are computed. Re-read the verdict if the corpus or the candidate definition changes.
- A top-1 'hit' requires a strictly best score; with mid-ranks a two-way tie for first gives rank 1.5 and counts as a top-3 hit only.
- The fitted rules were selected on MRR over small grids; the two halves have about 160 items each, so differences of a few points between fitted rules are within noise.
